# Romans (ROM)

>>>>>>>>>>>
In Romans, Paul is making an argument about the way that Gentiles, who are not obeying the law that God gave to Moses, can be included in the church alongside Jews who were still obeying that law. Things to note:
1. Faith: Typically, it is best practice to translate the same original language word in different ways, depending on its context. However, I have always translated the pist* word group as some form of "faith" so that it shows the continuity of Paul's argument. The noun can mean "belief," "trust," "loyalty," "faithfulness," or "allegiance." The verb usually means "believe" or "trust," but Paul is obviously using it to call to mind the same concept as the noun. A good case can be made that Paul's meta-concept is something like "allegiance." However, no English word is quite as flexible and equivalent as "faith," even though that word can be easily misunderstood. It's necessary to see the way Paul threads this word through his argument. I've footnoted the word that it would most easily translate as.
2. Law: usually, when Paul speaks of "law," he means the Law of Moses, which is apparent because of the attention he gives to his Jewish audience (though Gentiles are also in his audience). In Greek, when there is only one of something, the article "the" is often omitted, which is why it needs to be added in English.
3. Deeds: Paul contrasts deeds (usually translated "works") of the law of Moses with the loyalty (faith) of Christ and God's true followers. Since the word "work" is not usually used in normal speech in this way, I've tended toward words like "deed" and "perform."
4. Nations/Gentiles: The word translated "nations" or "Gentiles" is the same word. I haven't tried to use the same word to translate it, but translate it based on context---after all, it's fairly obvious that the two concepts are related, even in English.
5. Righteousness/justice/justification: The noun δικαιοσύνη and the adjective δίκαιος refer to righteousness/justice. The verb δικαιόω means to justify---make or consider righteous/just. The noun δικαίωσιν means justification---acquittal, process of absolution. Unfortunately, there's no English verb that quite has the range of δικαιόω except for "justify," but in my opinion when people hear "justify" in a doctrinal context, they don't hear its natural range of meaning, but the meaning given to it by theologians, so I've chosen not to use it. I've used "acquitted." Options other than "acquitted" could be judged righteous, pronounced righteous, declared righteous.
6. Accounted/imputed/considered: this is just the regular word for "consider \[something as\] \[something\]." However, Paul uses it as a central word in his argument, having to do with whether sins or righteousness are considered for or against someone. I have used "credited."
